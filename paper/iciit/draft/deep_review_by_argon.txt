# HIGH LEVEL SUMMARY
---
## Summary
This paper introduces the Tabular Transformation Discovery Harness, an autonomous, two-tier evaluation framework designed to benchmark Large Language Model (LLM) agents on inverse tabular program induction across six structural Abstract Syntax Tree (AST) complexity classes and eight real-world domains ($N = 48$ tasks). The architecture enforces strict epistemic isolation by decoupling Tier 1—an interactive Read-Eval-Print Loop (REPL) discovery sandbox governed by an adaptive resource budget controller—from Tier 2, a black-box holdout evaluation container where synthesized Python functions (`transform(df)`) and frozen empirical scaler moments are verified out-of-sample. Through cross-paradigm evaluations of frontier agents (Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Gemini 3 Flash), a lightweight edge model (GPT-5.4 Nano), and a depth-3 combinatorial baseline, alongside a three-mode ablation study, the paper demonstrates that while frontier models achieve perfect conditional recovery when ground-truth operators are disclosed, interactive hypothesis search and budget-aware stopping policies remain the primary bottlenecks in autonomous tabular discovery.

## Strengths
* The two-tier epistemically isolated evaluation architecture is thoughtfully engineered, effectively combining an unmounted holdout test container, complexity-scaled adaptive budgets, and frozen training-set empirical moments ($\mu, \sigma^2, \min, \max$) for stateful scalers to prevent data leakage, pre-training memorization, and test-set distributional contamination.
* Formulating a balanced, full-factorial benchmark across six structural AST complexity classes and eight real-world tabular domains fills a meaningful gap between single-target symbolic regression and natural-language-guided data science code synthesis, requiring agents to simultaneously identify transformed columns among distractors and invert multi-column non-linear compositions without semantic hints.
* The three-mode ablation protocol and dual reporting of conditional versus penalized accuracy provide valuable empirical insights by cleanly disentangling programmatic code compilation (where frontier models reach 100% conditional accuracy with oracle operators) from interactive REPL hypothesis discovery (which drops to 3.6% accuracy without execution tools) and conservative agentic stopping heuristics.

## Weaknesses
* The narrative in Section 5.1 and the boldface formatting in Table 1 state that Claude 3.5 Sonnet achieves the highest overall Penalized Transformed Accuracy (0.575) and characterize the Depth-3 Symbolic Enumeration baseline as suffering a "complete combinatorial explosion" on Class 5 (0.375); please consider revising this framing, as Table 1 shows that the naive depth-3 brute-force baseline actually outperforms all evaluated LLM agents across overall Penalized Transformed Accuracy (0.653), Penalized Functional Accuracy (0.876), Operator F1 (0.872), and every complex multi-step class from Class 2 through Class 5 (0.375 vs. Claude's 0.312).
* Because even an unguided depth-3 combinatorial search outperforms the tested LLM agents on overall penalized accuracy, relying solely on brute-force enumeration leaves an empirical gap; the authors might consider benchmarking established evolutionary or modular symbolic regression frameworks and LLM-guided equation discovery methods, as well as discussing prior art in by-target tabular pipeline synthesis (Reference Paper: Interpretable Machine Learning for Science with PySR and SymbolicRegression.jl, Miles Cranmer, 2023; Reference Paper: AI Feynman: A physics-inspired method for symbolic regression, Silviu-Marian Udrescu and Max Tegmark, 2020; Reference Paper: LLM-SR: Scientific Equation Discovery via Programming with Large Language Models, Parshin Shojaee et al., 2025; Reference Paper: LLM-SRBench: A New Benchmark for Scientific Equation Discovery with Large Language Models, Parshin Shojaee et al., 2025; Reference Paper: Auto-Pipeline: Synthesizing Complex Data Pipelines By-Target Using Reinforcement Learning and Search, Junwen Yang, Yeye He, and Surajit Chaudhuri, 2021; Reference Paper: DA-Code: Agent Data Science Code Generation Benchmark for Large Language Models, Yiming Huang et al., 2024).
* Please consider clarifying several methodological ambiguities regarding evaluation and reproducibility: specifically, how white-box symbolic metrics ($\widehat{\mathcal{O}}$) reconcile static Python AST parsing and algebraic equivalences with the self-reported operator argument in `submit_solution(script, O)`, how the $N=24$ ablation subset in Table 2 was sampled (where baseline penalized accuracy rises from 0.422 to 0.500), and why the `Total / Macro Average` row in Table 3 reports task-weighted micro-averaged conditional accuracies rather than unweighted domain macro-averages.
* Several entries in the References section contain mismatched arXiv identifiers or inaccurate author lists (notably References [13], [17], [19], [23], and [24]), and formal citations for Gemini 3 Flash and GPT-5.4 Nano are omitted; please consider updating the bibliography to ensure full accuracy.

### [Introduction, Motivation & Related Work] (Pages: 1-2)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **Omission of Prior Art in By-Example and By-Target Tabular Pipeline Synthesis (Section 2.2):**
   - *Potential Concern:* In Section 2.2 (*Program Synthesis and Symbolic Regression*), the paper cites a general program synthesis survey [11] and states that *"classical discrete program synthesis fails to scale to continuous, non-linear tabular transformations involving high-dimensional feature interactions."* While this distinction regarding continuous, non-linear mathematical transformations is valid, Section 2 does not discuss the directly related body of literature on programming-by-example (PBE) and by-target data pipeline synthesis specifically designed for tabular data—such as *Auto-Pipeline* (Yang et al., PVLDB 2021), which formulates the same inverse problem of synthesizing multi-step tabular data pipelines from input-output tables ($\mathcal{D}_{\text{before}}, \mathcal{D}_{\text{after}}$) using reinforcement learning and search, or *Foofah* (Jin et al., SIGMOD 2017) and *FlashFill* (Gulwani, POPL 2011).
   - *Suggested Improvement:* Briefly discussing by-target and by-example tabular pipeline synthesis in Section 2.2 would provide a more complete picture of prior work on the $\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{D}_{\text{before}}) \approx \mathcal{D}_{\text{after}}$ problem and allow the author to explicitly contrast prior relational reshaping and syntactic string transformations against the continuous, parametric mathematical ASTs, stateful distribution scalers, and conditional branching evaluated in this work.

2. **Characterization of *Table-GPT* [17] and *Sui et al.* [26] as Strictly Forward Predictive Classifiers/Regressors (Section 2.1):**
   - *Potential Concern:* In Section 2.1 (*Tabular Representation Learning and LLMs*), the text groups *Table-GPT* [17] and *Sui et al.* [26] with *TabLLM* [12], *TabICL* [20], and *TabICLv2* [21], and then concludes in the second paragraph: *"However, all these existing foundation models operate exclusively in the forward predictive regime: given observed training examples, predict target labels for test instances."* While this characterization is accurate for supervised tabular classification/regression models like *TabLLM*, *TabICL*, and *TabICLv2*, *Table-GPT* [17] is instruction-tuned on a broader set of table manipulation tasks (including data transformation, data cleaning, and schema matching via example pairs) rather than exclusively predicting target labels for test instances, and *Sui et al.* [26] is a benchmark for structural table comprehension (e.g., cell lookup, row/column retrieval).
   - *Suggested Improvement:* Refine the distinction in Section 2.1 to note that while supervised tabular foundation models (*TabLLM*, *TabICL*, *TabICLv2*) focus on forward target label prediction, instruction-tuned table models (*Table-GPT*) and table comprehension benchmarks (*Sui et al.*) operate via prompted, text-to-text token generation on small table snippets rather than synthesizing and verifying executable, parametric Python transformation pipelines in an isolated REPL sandbox.

### [Problem Formulation, Complexity Taxonomy & Two-Tier Harness Methodology] (Pages: 3-4)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **Sensitivity of White-Box Symbolic Metrics to Algebraic Equivalences, Implementation Variations, and Operator Extraction (Sections 3.3.1 & 3.4.3, Equations 11–12):**
   A substantive potential concern in the evaluation methodology is how the white-box symbolic metrics—Operator F1 Score ($\text{F1}_{\text{op}}$, Equation 11) and Exact Sequence Accuracy ($\text{Acc}_{\text{seq}}$, Equation 12)—handle mathematically equivalent expressions, varied Python coding idioms, and the extraction of the predicted operator sequence $\widehat{\mathcal{O}}$:
   - *Algebraic Equivalences and Commutativity:* Equations (11) and (12) rely on exact multiset intersection ($|\widehat{\mathcal{O}} \cap \mathcal{O}^*|$) and exact per-column sequence equality ($\mathbb{1}_{\{\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_c = \mathcal{O}_c^*\}}$) against a single canonical ground-truth sequence $\mathcal{O}^*$. In continuous mathematical program synthesis, distinct symbolic operator compositions within $\Omega$ frequently produce identical functional mappings across the valid domain—for example, expressing `Square` ($x^2$) via binary multiplication $x \times x$, $\ln((x+1)^2)$ as $2\ln(x+1)$, $e^{x_i + x_j}$ as $e^{x_i} \cdot e^{x_j}$, or composing `MinMaxScaler` followed by `StandardScaler` (which reduces to `StandardScaler` due to affine invariance). Similarly, for independent unary operations (Class 1), commutative binary interactions ($x_i + x_j$ vs. $x_j + x_i$ in Class 3), and multi-branch DAG topological sorts (Class 5), linear operator ordering is not unique. Without algebraic canonicalization (e.g., via SymPy) prior to computing Equations (11) and (12), functionally exact programs ($m(c) = 1.0$) would be penalized when using an algebraically equivalent representation.
   - *Discrepancy Between Self-Reported $\mathcal{O}$ and AST-Extracted $\widehat{\mathcal{O}}$:* Section 3.3.1 (and Figure 1) states that `submit_solution(script, O)` requires the agent to pass an explicit list of identified symbolic operators $\mathcal{O}$ alongside the standalone Python script, whereas Section 3.4.3 states: *"We parse the submitted script into an AST and extract the executed mathematical operator sequence $\widehat{\mathcal{O}}$."* It is unclear whether $\widehat{\mathcal{O}}$ is taken from the agent's self-reported argument $\mathcal{O}$, extracted independently via static AST parsing of `transform(df)`, or reconciled from both. Furthermore, if extracted via AST parsing, a single canonical operation in $\Omega$ can be written using diverse Python/NumPy/Pandas idioms (e.g., `df['c'] ** 2` vs. `np.square(df['c'])`). In particular, because Section 3.1 mandates that empirical moments ($\mu, \sigma^2, \min, \max$) for `StandardScaler` and `MinMaxScaler` are fitted on $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train\_before}}$ and applied as frozen scalar constants in `transform(df)` during Tier 2 evaluation, an agent writing `(df['c'] - mu) / sigma` in Python uses elementary subtraction and division AST nodes (`Sub`, `Div`) rather than invoking a canonical `StandardScaler` operator token.
   - *Column-Level Mapping and Aggregation Scope:* Equation (11) defines $\text{F1}_{\text{op}}$ over a table-level multiset $\widehat{\mathcal{O}}$, whereas Equation (12) indexes $\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_c$ per transformed column $c \in \mathcal{C}_{\text{trans}}$. Clarifying how AST parsing maps arbitrary syntax to the 24 canonical tokens in $\Omega$, how global operators are attributed to specific columns $\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_c$, and whether $\text{F1}_{\text{op}}$ and $\text{Acc}_{\text{seq}}$ are aggregated conditionally on submitted tasks or penalized across all $N_{\text{total}}$ tasks is essential for reproducibility.

2. **Incomplete Specification of the 24 Canonical Operators $\Omega$ and Task Generation Hyperparameters (Sections 3.1, 3.2 & 4.1):**
   A potential concern for independent reproducibility of the benchmark tasks, interpretation of the accuracy metrics, and the combinatorial brute-force baseline search space ($\mathcal{O}(M^d \cdot |\Omega|^d)$) is that several generative specifications in Sections 3.1, 3.2, and 4.1 remain only partially disclosed:
   - *Complete Enumeration of $\Omega$:* Section 3.1 defines the transformation pipeline over a discrete vocabulary of 24 canonical operators $\Omega = \{\omega_1, \dots, \omega_{24}\}$, but only enumerates 16 specific operations in the text: 6 unary functions ($\ln(x+1), \sqrt{x}, x^2, x^3, 1/x, e^x$), 3 trigonometric/hyperbolic transforms ($\sin, \cos, \tanh$), 2 stateful scalers (`StandardScaler`, `MinMaxScaler`), 4 bivariate arithmetic interactions ($+, -, \times, \div$), and 1 piecewise indicator ($\mathbb{1}_{\{x > \theta\}} \cdot x$). Enumerating the remaining 8 operators in $\Omega$ (such as $\ln(x)$, which is mentioned later in Section 5.3.3) in a table or inline list is necessary so readers know the exact hypothesis space.
   - *Complexity Class Parameters and Number of Transformed Columns ($|\mathcal{C}_{\text{trans}}|$) (Sections 3.2 & 4.1):* Key structural hyperparameters across the six classes are not specified: (i) for Class 1 through Class 5, how many columns $k = |\mathcal{C}_{\text{trans}}|$ are modified per task (because the gap between $\text{Acc}_{\text{trans}}$ and $\text{Acc}_{\text{func}}$ depends directly on the ratio $|\mathcal{C}_{\text{trans}}| / |\mathcal{C}_{\text{after}}|$, specifying $|\mathcal{C}_{\text{trans}}|$ per class is essential for interpreting the reported metrics across datasets ranging from 4 to 30 columns); (ii) for Class 2, the composition chain depth $K$ (whether strictly depth 2 as in $f_2(f_1(x))$ or higher); (iii) for Class 4, the rule for selecting the splitting threshold $\theta$ (e.g., sample median, specific quantile of $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train\_before}}$, or zero-crossing) so that both branches have sufficient support and $\theta$ is identifiable without continuous optimization, as well as how the general two-branch formulation in Equation (4) relates to the single indicator primitive $\mathbb{1}_{\{x > \theta\}} \cdot x$ in Section 3.1; and (iv) for Class 5, the DAG depth, branching factor, and number of modified/added columns.
   - *Train/Test Split Sizes ($N_{\text{train}}, N_{\text{test}}$) and Split Regime (Sections 3.1 & 4.1):* Section 3.1 introduces $N_{\text{train}}$ and $N_{\text{test}}$, and Section 1 (Item 4) states that the synthesized `transform(df)` function must generalize "out-of-distribution." However, the paper does not report the split ratios or sample sizes for $N_{\text{train}}$ and $N_{\text{test}}$, nor does it clarify whether $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$ and $\mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}$ are partitioned via uniform random (i.i.d.) sampling or covariate-shifted/extrapolative splitting (e.g., splitting along feature quantiles so test values lie outside the training support).
   - *Column Schema Alignment and Naming ($\mathcal{C}_{\text{before}} \to \mathcal{C}_{\text{after}}$):* Equation (1) allows the column count to change from $M$ to $P$ ($\mathcal{C}_{\text{after}} = \{\tilde{c}_1, \dots, \tilde{c}_P\}$), Class 3 generates a new column $z = x_i \star x_j$, and Section 3.4.1 states that *"unaligned columns receive $m(c) = 0$."* However, the text does not explain how columns in $\mathcal{C}_{\text{after}}$ are named—specifically, whether modified columns in Classes 0–2 and 4 overwrite original names in-place and whether newly created columns in Classes 3 and 5 append new columns ($P > M$) using anonymized identifiers (e.g., `col_new_1`) to avoid leaking the source columns $x_i, x_j$ or operators via the column header.
   - *Precondition Enforcement Mechanism (Section 4.1):* Section 4.1 states that tasks were generated "ensuring that transformations satisfied domain-specific mathematical preconditions (e.g., non-negative domains for square roots, positive values for logarithms, non-zero divisors for divisions)." It is unclear whether this was enforced via rejection sampling (restricting candidate operators to columns whose raw distributions naturally satisfy those domain constraints) or by shifting/clipping the underlying column values prior to applying $\mathcal{T}$.

3. **Alignment Between the Formal Optimization Objective, Prompt Specifications, and the Functional Evaluation Criterion (Sections 3.1, 3.4.1 & 5.1.1, Equations 3, 8, & 9):**
   A potential concern regarding mathematical consistency exists between the formal problem objective in Equation (3), the agent's REPL verification threshold in Section 5.1.1, and the Tier 2 evaluation metric in Equations (8)–(9):
   - Equation (3) formulates the objective as minimizing the expected infinity norm of the absolute error across features: $\min_{\widehat{\mathcal{T}}} \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x} \sim \mathcal{D}_{\text{test\_before}}} \left[\|\widehat{\mathcal{T}}(\mathbf{x}) - \mathcal{T}(\mathbf{x})\|_\infty\right]$ (and Section 5.1.1 later notes that Gemini 3 Flash continues iterating when training residual error does not reach numerical tolerance $\|\widehat{\mathcal{D}} - \mathcal{D}\|_\infty < 10^{-6}$).
   - In contrast, Equation (8) evaluates functional accuracy column-by-column using a scale-normalized, mixed relative-absolute tolerance $\epsilon = 10^{-4}$: $|\widehat{D}_{i,c} - D_{i,c}| < 10^{-4} \cdot (|D_{i,c}| + 1)$, and Equation (9) marks a column correct if $m(c) \ge 0.99$ (allowing up to $1\%$ of test rows, such as boundary points near threshold $\theta$ or extreme tails, to exceed tolerance).
   - Because an unnormalized $\ell_\infty$ norm across heterogeneous columns is dominated by whichever feature has the largest numerical magnitude (e.g., after $e^x$ or $x^3$ transformations), requiring an absolute residual below $10^{-6}$ is orders of magnitude stricter than the scale-normalized criterion in Equation (8). Conversely, on features with very small magnitudes ($|x| \ll 1$), the $+1$ term in Equation (8) relaxes the criterion to an absolute tolerance of $10^{-4}$, where distinct functions with identical first-order Taylor expansions near zero (such as $\sin(x)$, $\tanh(x)$, and $\ln(1+x)$) can satisfy $m(c) \ge 0.99$ without recovering the true operator. Aligning Equation (3) with Equation (8)—and clarifying in Sections 3.3/3.4 and 5.1.1 whether $10^{-6}$ is the agent's self-imposed convergence threshold during REPL verification or whether agents are explicitly informed in their system prompt of the $\epsilon = 10^{-4}$ and $m(c) \ge 0.99$ pass criteria and the requirement to hardcode frozen training moments $(\mu, \sigma^2, \min, \max)$ rather than calling `.fit_transform(df)` at test time—would make the problem formulation and stopping analysis more cohesive.

4. **Execution Semantics of the Tier 1 REPL Subprocess, Budget Accounting, and Tier 2 Sandbox Limits (Sections 3.3.1–3.3.3 & 3.4.2):**
   Several systems-level specifications in the Two-Tier Epistemic Isolation Harness warrant clarification for reproducibility:
   - *Tier 1 REPL State Persistence (Section 3.3.1):* `run_python(code)` is described both as an *"interactive Read-Eval-Print Loop (REPL)"* and as executing in *"a sanitized subprocess with stripped host environment variables... and an enforced 15-second timeout."* In standard Python architectures, spawning a fresh subprocess per tool invocation is stateless (destroying in-memory variables, imports, and DataFrames between turns unless state serialization or a persistent kernel daemon is used), whereas a true REPL maintains persistent in-memory state across calls. Clarifying whether in-memory variables persist across consecutive `run_python` calls or if only filesystem artifacts (such as diagnostic plots saved for `read_image`) persist would make the Tier 1 execution loop unambiguous.
   - *Token Budget Accounting and Metric Aggregation (Sections 3.3.2 & 3.4.2):* In Equation (6), the adaptive budget specifies $K_{\text{tokens}}(c) \in [60\text{k}, \dots, 300\text{k}]$. In multi-turn agent loops where conversation history is re-sent at each turn, cumulative input tokens across turns grow quadratically with episode length, whereas peak context window length grows linearly; specifying whether $K_{\text{tokens}}(c)$ caps cumulative billed tokens (input + output across all turns) or maximum context window tokens would remove ambiguity. Similarly, in Section 3.4.2 (Equation 10), explicitly stating that benchmark-wide $\text{Acc}_{\text{cond}}$ is defined as the unweighted macro-average of per-task accuracies over submitted tasks ($\frac{1}{N_{\text{sub}}} \sum_{i \in \text{submitted}} \text{Acc}_i$) ensures that the factorization $\text{Acc}_{\text{pen}} = \text{Submission Rate} \times \text{Acc}_{\text{cond}}$ holds exactly when $|\mathcal{C}_{\text{trans}}|$ varies across classes.
   - *Tier 2 Runtime Limits and Static AST Validation (Section 3.3.3):* Unlike Tier 1 (which specifies a 15s per-call timeout and class-dependent budgets in Equations 5–7), Section 3.3.3 does not state the execution timeout or memory limit enforced on the Tier 2 container when executing `transform(df)` on $\mathcal{D}_{\text{test\_before}}$. Additionally, while AST validation for dangerous calls (`os.system`, `subprocess`, network sockets) is framed as a defense-in-depth barrier alongside container isolation, static AST blocklisting in Python is easily bypassed via dynamic reflection (e.g., `getattr`, `__builtins__`, `eval`), so security guarantees should rely explicitly on the OS-level container and filesystem/network isolation.

### [Experimental Setup, Benchmark Tasks & Baseline Configurations] (Pages: 4-5)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **Absence of Heuristic or Evolutionary Symbolic Regression Baselines Relative to Cited Frameworks (Abstract, Section 1, Section 2.2, Section 4.2, Section 5.1.2 & Section 6.2):**
   - **Potential Concern:** Section 2.2 explicitly highlights that modern symbolic regression frameworks such as *PySR* [6], *AI Feynman* [27], and *LLM-SR* [23] utilize Pareto-optimal evolutionary search, physics-inspired modular decomposition, or LLM-guided evolutionary program search *"to avoid exhaustive combinatorial enumeration."* However, in the Abstract, Section 1 (Contribution 4), and Section 4.2, the sole non-LLM symbolic baseline included in the empirical benchmark is the "Symbolic Baseline (Combinatorial Brute Force)," which performs unguided, exhaustive enumeration of candidate unary and binary operator combinations up to depth $d = 3$. While this baseline illustrates the exponential scaling of unguided enumeration ($\mathcal{O}(M^d \cdot |\Omega|^d)$) on Class 5 tasks (0.375 accuracy) and is acknowledged as a limitation in Section 6.2 (Limitation 4), relying solely on depth-3 brute-force enumeration provides a weak classical comparison point for deeper complexity classes (such as Class 2 and Class 5) where AST depth reaches or exceeds $d = 3$. Without evaluating a competitive heuristic or evolutionary symbolic regression baseline, it is difficult to assess whether autonomous ReAct REPL agents outperform modern symbolic discovery algorithms under equivalent wall-clock budgets (120s–360s), or if the reported baseline collapse on Class 5 is strictly an artifact of naive depth-3 enumeration.
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Consider including an empirical comparison in Section 4.2 against an established heuristic or evolutionary symbolic regression package (e.g., running PySR column-wise on $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train\_before}} \to \mathcal{D}_{\text{train\_after}}$ under the same wall-clock time budgets $\Delta t_{\text{wall}}(c)$) or LLM-guided equation discovery framework (e.g., LLM-SR), or explicitly clarify upfront in Section 1, Section 2.2, Section 4.2, and Section 6.2 if specific structural properties of the benchmark—such as simultaneous multi-column schema mutations, stateful train-to-test distribution scalers (`StandardScaler`/`MinMaxScaler`), indicator thresholds, or unprompted column discovery—prevented direct execution of standard single-target symbolic regression packages without task-specific wrapper heuristics.

2. **Missing Specification of the Symbolic Baseline's Handling of Conditional Thresholds, Stateful Scalers, and Compute Budgets (Section 4.2):**
   - **Potential Concern:** Section 4.2 defines the Symbolic Baseline as a classical algorithm that "iteratively evaluates all candidate unary and binary operator combinations up to depth $d = 3$, using residual $L_1$ norm minimization with early pruning." However, this description does not explain how a discrete combinatorial search over the 24 operators handles continuous parameters—most notably the splitting threshold $\theta$ required for Class 4 (Piecewise / Conditional Logic, Equation 4), where the baseline achieves its highest penalized accuracy ($0.812$ in Table 1, outperforming all three LLM agents)—nor how it incorporates stateful distribution scalers (`StandardScaler`, `MinMaxScaler`). Furthermore, the early-pruning threshold and the compute/timeout budget allocated to the baseline relative to the Adaptive Tiered Budget Controller (Equations 5–7) are not specified, leaving it unclear whether the baseline's collapse on Class 5 is primarily due to the fixed depth limit ($d = 3$) or memory/timeout exhaustion.
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Specify in Section 4.2 how candidate thresholds $\theta$ are discretized, sampled (e.g., via quantile grid search or empirical mean/median splits), or optimized during the combinatorial search; clarify how stateful scalers are parameterized within the candidate grammar; and state the exact early-pruning criterion and wall-clock/memory limits enforced on the baseline per complexity class.

3. **Incomplete Details on the Ablation Study Task Subset and Mode Configurations (Section 4.3 & Section 5.2):**
   - **Potential Concern:** Section 4.3 defines the three experimental modes for the ablation study, which is evaluated on a subset of **$N = 24$ tasks** in Section 5.2 and Table 2 (`Submissions: 14/24`, `13/24`, `16/24`) rather than the full $N = 48$ benchmark suite evaluated in Table 1 and Table 3. However, Sections 4.3 and 5.2 leave several operational details of the ablation protocol ambiguous:
     - **Selection of the $N = 24$ Subset:** Under the `Standard Interactive Discovery (Full Agent)` condition in Table 2, Gemini 3 Flash achieves a $54.2\%$ submission rate ($13/24$), `0.923` Conditional Transformed Accuracy ($12/13$), `0.500` Penalized Transformed Accuracy ($12/24$), and `0.861` Operator F1, which is noticeably higher than its performance on the full $N = 48$ benchmark in Table 1 ($50.0\%$ submission rate, $24/48$, `0.844` Conditional, `0.422` Penalized, `0.853` Operator F1—implying $11/24$ submissions and $8.25/11 = 0.750$ conditional accuracy on the excluded 24 tasks). Neither Section 4.3 nor Section 5.2 specifies how these 24 tasks were selected from the 48 benchmark tasks (e.g., whether 4 of the 8 domains were evaluated across all 6 complexity classes, 3 complexity classes across all 8 domains, or a stratified random half-split).
     - **Tool Availability and Format of $\mathcal{O}^*$ in Mode 3 (*Oracle Operators Revealed*):** Section 4.3 states that "the exact ground-truth operator names $\mathcal{O}^*$ are provided in the prompt" and that the agent's task is "strictly reduced to mapping the known operators to columns and generating syntactically valid code."
